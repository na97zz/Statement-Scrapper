Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
Protecting independence while embracing partnership 
Tēnā koutou katoa. 
It’s great to be back again to open proceedings at the Directors and Senior Officers (DSO) 
workshop for our regulated entities. As my last time as Governor, I thought it fitting to share some 
reflections on central bank independence after nearly 30 years in and around central banking.1   
In the process, I want to: 
 
Reaffirm the crucial importance of protecting operational independence. 
 
Explain that central bank independence does not have to mean working in isolation. In my 
view, a clear division of roles and responsibilities between government and central bank  
can enable an effective partnership, ultimately supporting prosperity and wellbeing of  
New Zealanders.  
 
Argue that the need for partnership extends much beyond government and central bank, into 
other regulators, the financial industry, fintechs, academics, business sector, communities, iwi 
and a range of other government agencies. This will be crucial to successfully shape the future 
of money, the future of cash, the future of banking, the future of payments and the future of 
insurance. 
Introduction 
You do not have to look far to see central bank independence under attack.   
Ten years ago, it would be unheard of for a US President to openly disagree with interest rate 
policy or call the US Federal Reserve Chairman a string of names and insults. It has now become 
commonplace. The worry amongst the global central banking community is that this rubs off and 
becomes normalised internationally. 
The period of the 1990s and 2000s was probably when central bank independence had the most 
support. This was the period of the so-called Great Moderation of low and stable consumer price 
inflation, and long unbroken periods of real economic expansion.   
That level of support and acceptance has been eroded since the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) and 
the Covid-19 pandemic. Through these periods there have been episodes of lower growth and 
higher inflation; a perceived blurring of lines between monetary policy and fiscal policy (especially 
through central bank balance sheet expansion); and the cost of living has become an increasingly 
political issue globally.

Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
Operational independence for monetary policy 
Taking a step back, it is useful to remind ourselves why operational independence for monetary 
policy is important and how it came about. 
It deals with the so-called time inconsistency problem when politicians set monetary policy.    
That is, even if politicians say that they want monetary policy formulated to achieve low and stable 
inflation, they have the incentive to boost economic growth ahead of an election to get re-elected 
at the expense of higher inflation. This incentive to “cheat” undermines their promise. As a result, 
inflation expectations remain elevated to factor in the chance of high inflation, and this is reflected 
in wage and price setting behaviour. Periods of high and variable inflation are also damaging for 
long-term economic growth prospects. This is the story of the 1970s and 1980s.  
Providing operational independence to a credible central bank to formulate monetary policy takes 
away this incentive “to cheat” and so helps anchor inflation expectations. While central banks like 
the Bundesbank, the Swiss National Bank, the US Federal Reserve had become increasingly 
independent, New Zealand was the first country to formalise operational independence into 
legislation, as part of the first inflation targeting regime introduced in 1990. Both elements have 
since been adopted around the world. 
At the same time, in New Zealand a framework for fiscal responsibility was introduced, to ensure 
that monetary policy had friends in the form of fiscal policy focused on long-term sustainability. In 
practice, a key element of this has been self-imposed fiscal rules for a target level and trajectory of 
government debt to GDP.   
Operational independence not isolation 
It is equally important to remind ourselves that we are talking about “operational independence”, 
not complete autonomy or isolation. 
Monetary policy is most effective when there are clear, transparent, measurable targets, and a high 
degree of credibility in the targets. Having these objectives set by elected officials is important for 
democratic legitimacy and promotes long-run credibility of the regime. In New Zealand, the 
Minister of Finance is the minister responsible for the Reserve Bank and its legislation.   
In that sense, politicians have a critical role to play in monetary policy: owning the legislation 
(Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 2021), setting the policy objective, and outlining these in detail 
in the Monetary Policy Remit. In these aspects, the Minister of Finance is the decision maker, with 
the Reserve Bank providing advice. 
These objectives need to be clear, quantitative, and measurable. Ideally, in my view, the objective 
needs to be hierarchical, with inflation above all other considerations, so there is no question that 
low and stable medium-term inflation will be compromised to achieve other short-term 
objectives.2

Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
Once these targets are set, it is crucial that the Reserve Bank has, and is perceived to have, full 
operational independence to pursue these objectives, including appropriate financial 
arrangements that allow the room to respond to a range of circumstances with the appropriate 
tools. I can recommend the recent speech by my colleague, Paul Conway, as an excellent account 
of how independent monetary policy and fiscal policy can work alongside each other, both in 
peace times and periods of crisis.3   
At the same time, the huge responsibility of having operational independence for monetary policy 
decisions needs to go hand in hand with transparency and accountability. This occurs through a 
range of channels, including the intense scrutiny of Monetary Policy Statements, publications, 
research, projections, press conferences, speeches, parliamentary hearings, media coverage, Board 
governance oversight, monitoring from the Treasury, and scheduled reviews.    
This emphasis on accountability is important because one of the major political benefits of an 
operationally independent central bank is distancing themselves from the decisions. That is, the 
government not taking the blame from voters for the short-term pain that can be caused by 
monetary policy – e.g. when interest rates have to rise to keep inflation down – for the medium- to 
long-term economic gain. And vice versa on the other side of the economic cycle. 
Operational independence and financial stability 
The Reserve Bank of New Zealand is a so-called full service central bank, responsible for monetary 
policy and financial stability, including the prudential regulation of deposit takers, insurers and 
financial market infrastructures.  
A sound and well-functioning financial system – encompassing markets, institutions, and payment 
systems – serves as a public good, much like physical infrastructures such as transport, energy, 
water, and communications, which benefit society broadly beyond individual users. At the same 
time, we know that financial systems can be inherently fragile, vulnerable and highly 
interconnected, meaning a failure in one part can quickly spread and endanger the wider system 
as a whole. This is what motivates the public policy case for a resilient financial system. 
There is much less discussion in the popular media about operational independence when it 
comes to the responsibility of central banks for financial stability. There are some parallels with 
monetary policy, but also many important differences that I will come to later. 
The first similarity is there is a time inconsistency problem if decisions were left solely with 
politicians. Elected officials could have the incentive “to cheat” by relaxing prudential settings at 
politically convenient times to boost economic growth, potentially to the detriment of overall 
financial stability at a later stage. This is what motivates the need for operational independence for 
the long-run credibility and continuity of the regime, to support trust and confidence in there 
being a resilient financial system.

Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
Again, to ensure democratic legitimacy and promote long-run credibility, an elected official in the 
form of the Minister of Finance needs to ultimately own the legislation underpinning the Reserve 
Bank’s responsibility for financial stability. On these foundations of the regime, the Minister of 
Finance is the decision maker, with the Reserve Bank providing advice. 
In New Zealand, this is covered by a range of legislation that captures the purposes, principles and 
policy objectives set for the prudential regulator.4 The Financial Policy Remit outlines issues that the 
Minister considers are desirable for the financial system. These priorities are generally consistent 
with the government’s other objectives. For example, the current Financial Policy Remit has a 
strong emphasis on competition and innovation. The Reserve Bank must have regard to the 
Financial Policy Remit when performing its prudential role, such as when it is issuing prudential 
standards.   
Similarly to setting monetary policy, operational independence as a prudential regulator and 
supervisor creates the space for technicians to design and implement prudential standards, and for 
them to be supervised and enforced against without scope for political inference. It creates the 
arm’s length to keep politicians safe, particular on matters relating to individual institutions, 
providing the ability for them to say, “you need to speak to the regulator”. While the Reserve Bank 
as the “resolution authority” is responsible for resolution planning and, if necessary, conducting the 
resolution of a failed institution, given the possible systemic impact of a resolution action and the 
potential for the use of public funds the Governor General on advice of the Minister of Finance 
retains the final decision as to whether an institution is placed into resolution. 
Like setting monetary policy, operational independence also needs to go hand in hand with a high 
degree of transparency and accountability. This comes through accountability documents like 
Financial Stability Reports, research, press conferences, speeches, parliamentary hearings, media 
coverage, Board governance oversight, monitoring from the Treasury, the extensive use of 
consultations with the industry on regulatory changes, the publication of regulatory impact 
statements for significant changes, and the use of independent experts to provide advice and 
external scrutiny.

In my experience, this sense of partnership needs to be even stronger when it comes to financial 
stability, for several reasons: 
 
Financial stability is much harder to define than monetary stability.6 As such, it is not well suited 
to a quantitative measure that can be used for transparency, accountability and legitimacy in 
the same way as a numerical 1 to 3 percent inflation target for monetary policy.  
 
A resilient financial system is just one enabler of long-term prosperity and wellbeing. Having a 
financial system that is competitive, efficient, innovative and inclusive are also important 
enablers. As such, there is more potential for trade-offs when setting prudential policy, 
compared with monetary policy where medium-term inflation has a clearly paramount 
position.

Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
 
Policy choices involve a range of considerations and have potential implications for economic 
efficiency, distributional and fiscal outcomes down the track. It is not always easy to set these 
considerations and relevant trade-offs in a Financial Policy Remit. However, that mechanism 
allows an elected Minister to be more involved in those “risk appetite” judgement calls and 
trade-offs, within an agreed framework.         
 
Finally, a key policy tool in crisis management is the potential use of the Crown balance sheet 
in any financial institution or system rescue, which is ultimately the responsibility of the Minister 
of Finance. In crisis management, the Reserve Bank again becomes a key adviser given its 
expertise, systemic risk assessment, and operational capacity to provide market liquidity. But 
the Minister of Finance and government must make the final calls on deployment or otherwise 
of public funds to mitigate the effects of a bank or financial system failure.    
Within this framework of working in partnership on financial stability, at times there will be natural 
tensions between the central bank and Minister of Finance, given the different roles, incentives and 
perspectives. In my view, these need to be dealt with early and transparently, to provide both 
democratic legitimacy and give the public trust and confidence in the long-term resilience of the 
financial system.  
Partnership with regulators and industry 
Of course, the sense of working in partnership needs to extend beyond central bank and Minister. 
In New Zealand, we have a twin peaks regulatory model with the Reserve Bank the prudential 
regulator and Financial Markets Authority (FMA) the conduct regulator.   
Together, the Reserve Bank and FMA co-chair the Council of Financial Regulators (COFR), with the 
other members being the Treasury, MBIE and the Commerce Commission. COFR members work 
together to facilitate co-operation and co-ordination between members to support effective and 
responsive regulation of the financial system in New Zealand. Collectively, these agencies have 
tools that all contribute to having a financial system that is resilient, competitive, efficient, 
innovative, and inclusive.  
Internationally, given the connection with the Australian financial system, we work in partnership as 
part of the Trans-Tasman Banking Council (TTBC), comprising the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA), 
Australia Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA), Australian Securities and Investments 
Commission (ASIC) and Australian Treasury; with RBNZ, FMA, and NZ Treasury. Trans-Tasman 
crisis preparedness is a key aspect of providing trust and confidence in our financial systems.  
The need for partnership extends even further than our region of the world.   
Globally, the financial system is changing rapidly with the evolving preferences of customers, new 
entrants and providers of financial services, new instruments such as cryptocurrencies, stablecoins 
and central bank digital currencies (CBDC), new real-time retail payments platforms, and new 
technologies such as the tokenisation of assets and the programmability of payments. The Bank 
for International Settlements (BIS) Innovation Hub has been at the forefront of preparing for this 
new world, while ensuring that central bank money and money sovereignty remains at its core.7

Protecting independence while embracing partnership  
There are areas where New Zealand’s financial system is being left behind internationally, 
especially in real-time retail payments. Countries like India (with the Unified Payments Interface, 
UPI) and Brazil (with the Pix instant payments system) have leapfrogged into a new generation of 
technology, unlocking significant economic efficiencies and opportunities. Our neighbours in 
Southeast Asia are at the forefront of real-time, low cost, cross-border retail payments. The EMEAP 
(Executives’ Meeting of East Asia Pacific Central Banks) has become an invaluable forum to share 
and learn from the experiences of our neighbours.  
In New Zealand, we need to work together with our COFR colleagues, the financial industry, 
fintechs, academics, the business sector, communities, iwi and a range of other government 
agencies on the future of money, the future of cash, the future of banking, the future of payments 
and the future of insurance. The benefits to all New Zealanders will not be achieved without 
collective commitment and collective action.  
While the Reserve Bank needs to continue protecting its operational independence for monetary 
policy decisions and setting prudential standards, we also need to embrace partnership to achieve 
our ultimate purpose of promoting the prosperity and wellbeing of New Zealanders. 
Nāu te rourou, nāku te rourou, ka ora ai te iwi.   
With our contribution and your contribution, the people will prosper.
